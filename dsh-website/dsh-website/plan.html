The concept

Your two brochures describe many services. One thing ties them together: the screen on the wall that runs the lights, curtains, AC, cameras and intercom. Your profile's own line is «تحكم بجميع غرف منزلك بشاشة واحدة فقط», “control every room from one screen.” The website should make that promise visible.

So the hero product is the MixPad 7 Ultra smart panel in the walnut finish, from your partner ORVIBO. It gets more pages in your brochure than any other product. The feature worth revealing is not a chip inside it. It is what the panel connects to: the wired modules in the electrical board and, through them, every device in the room. This also covers your weak spot: we have no documents on the panel's internals, so we will not invent any.

Why this product

Every DSH project has one. The customer touches it every day. It is the one object that explains “integrated solutions under one roof,” your own selling point.

Why walnut

Your brochure shows three finishes: silver, walnut and green. Walnut sits naturally next to travertine, plaster and warm lamp light, the materials of the room we land in.

One thing to settle first

The panel is ORVIBO's design. Before we show its name or an exact likeness, get ORVIBO's written OK and, ideally, its official CAD files or renders. Without them, we show a panel with the same proportions and no logo, and call it “the panel we install.”

How it stays one shot: we plan the camera path in Blender and export it, so the website plays one path from start to end. Parts only move along one main direction (with a small arc), which keeps the separation easy to follow.

The room: a family majlis in a Riyadh villa

I chose the family majlis (المجلس العائلي) over a bedroom or kitchen. It is where Saudi families host guests, so lighting, curtains, AC and the front door all matter at the same moment. It also matches your brochure's villa photography and the panel's built-in “Guests” scene. The style is contemporary Najdi: clean geometry, local stone, warm plaster and desert light, without ornament overload.

Materials

  • Floor: large beige travertine-look porcelain tiles (120 × 60 cm) under a wool rug with a Sadu-pattern border.
  • Walls: warm white lime plaster. The feature wall is fluted walnut with a travertine ledge.
  • Seating: low, wide U-shaped majlis sofas in oat linen, plus two accent chairs.
  • Accents: brushed brass, matte ceramic, one carved-wood side table.

Lighting (each group can be dimmed on its own)

  • Gypsum ceiling with a hidden LED cove all around, 2700K. This is very common in Saudi villas.
  • Six small downlights, a wash light on the walnut wall, one floor lamp.
  • Daylight from the west window: harsh in the late afternoon, glowing at Maghrib.
  • TV glow and the panel's own screen as small accent lights.

Everyday details that make it feel lived in

  • A dallah, a thermos and small finjan cups on a tray, with dates in a covered bowl.
  • A tissue box on the side table and a phone charging on the arm of the sofa.
  • A mabkhara with a thin line of oud smoke (decorative; turned off for reduced motion).
  • A slightly creased cushion, a folded throw, the TV remote and the AC remote left out.
  • The panel's home screen shows the next prayer time, “Maghrib 6:22.”

Simulator walkthrough: guests arriving after Maghrib

One complete interaction. The story is real for any Saudi household: it's a hot summer afternoon and guests are coming after Maghrib.

Start in a real moment

The room opens at 5:40 pm in August (example values). A small note says: “Guests arrive after Maghrib. Get the majlis ready.” The panel by the door shows the time, 41° outside, and the next prayer time.

The customer taps one scene

They tap Guests (ضيوف) on the panel itself, or on a phone-app view of the same controls. These are the scene buttons from your brochure, translated into Arabic.

The room responds in a visible order (about 4 seconds)

Sheer curtains glide shut and the glare streak on the rug fades. The cove light rises to a warm 70%, the walnut wall gets its wash light, downlights drop to 30%. The AC target changes to 22°, shown on the panel. The entrance light comes on and a “Front door camera ready” tile appears. Each change starts a beat after the last, so the eye can follow it.

They explore

A time slider moves the day from Asr to Maghrib to Isha. As the window darkens, the blackout curtain closes too and the lights rebalance. Switches let them remove a device, for example “without curtain motors.” The glare comes straight back, so they see exactly what that device does.

They leave with a list, not a vague idea

A side list updates as they play: the devices this scene needs, each with one line on what it does. Tapping an item highlights it in the room. The main button says Book a site survey for this room, which is step 2 of your own process.

Continuous shot: the hero's last frame is the simulator's first frame. Same camera, same lighting, same room. Alexandria is the Arabic companion to Montserrat, and your logo's English wordmark is set in a Montserrat-style face. So headlines in both languages feel related to the logo. Light weight at large sizes, Medium for small headings. No letter-spacing on Arabic: it breaks the joins.

These icon tiles look like the panel's own glass buttons: frosted glass, a thin bright top edge, simple line icons. When a device is on, the glass picks up its color: warm Lamp for lights, cool Wire for AC. Each icon stands for a real device from your brochure.

Glass goes here

  • The panel's cover glass in 3D: real reflections of the room.
  • The window: the sheer curtain softens the view like frosted glass.
  • Simulator controls, because they are the panel's screen, pulled forward.
  • The phone-app control sheet on mobile.

We avoid

  • Glass on ordinary content (services, process, contact). Those sections sit flat on Night or Walnut.
  • Purple-blue glows, neon edges, floating cards everywhere.
  • Decorative 3D shapes. The only 3D objects are the panel, its system and the room.
  • Capital-letter labels with dots (“OUR SERVICES ·”) and numbered 01/02 markers, except on your real 6-step process.

Phones and reduced motion

On phones

  • The hero tells the same story in 5 beats, with text under the picture rather than on top of it.
  • Newer phones get the live 3D scene. Older phones, or phones in data-saver mode, get a pre-rendered frame sequence that scrolls the same way and looks the same.
  • The simulator camera stays fixed at the seated view. Controls slide up as a bottom sheet styled like the DSH phone app, because that's how customers will really control their home.
  • Large tap targets (at least 44 px), one-thumb use, and the room loads only when the visitor reaches it.

With reduced motion turned on

  • No camera flight or zoom. The hero becomes 5 still pictures that fade into each other, with “Next part” buttons.
  • Simulator changes apply straight away with a short fade. Curtains appear closed instead of sliding.
  • Decorative motion is removed: incense smoke, the slow turn, the travelling light on the wire.
  • Every beat and every change also exists as text, and a pause button is always visible.

Motion rule for the whole site: the cinematic hero is seen once, so it can take its time. Scene changes are the demo, so they're staged over a few seconds. Button taps answer within a tenth of a second and never wait for an animation.
